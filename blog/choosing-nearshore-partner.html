What to Look for in a Nearshore Engineering Partner (and What to Avoid)

The difference between a good nearshore partner and a bad one is not obvious from a sales call. Here is how to tell them apart.

RM
Rashad M.
Co-Founder, Resourcee

The nearshore staffing market has grown significantly in the past five years, and with growth comes variance. There are companies doing genuinely excellent work: rigorous vetting, deep candidate networks, serious ongoing support. And there are body shops that use the nearshore label but operate like CV distributors: low vetting, high volume, low accountability.

The difference between working with the right partner and the wrong one can mean the difference between a thriving nearshore team and a series of disappointing placements. Here is how to tell them apart.

The Technical Vetting Question

The single most important thing to understand about a nearshore partner is how they assess technical skills. Ask them to describe their technical screening process in detail. Not just that they do it, but exactly what it looks like.

A serious answer will include: the format of the technical assessment (live coding, take-home, or both), the criteria they use to evaluate it, who conducts the technical screen (an engineer or a recruiter), and how they calibrate their bar for different technology stacks and seniority levels.

A partner who cannot answer this specifically is one who does not have a serious vetting process. The answer you want to hear is something like: we have technical screeners who specialize in backend, frontend, and infrastructure. Our process includes a live technical interview, a practical coding challenge, and a system design discussion for senior roles. We calibrate our bar by stack because the standards for Go differ from the standards for Python.

The answer you do not want to hear is some version of: we review their portfolio and resume, and then you interview them. That is not vetting. That is forwarding.

The English Assessment Question

Ask how they evaluate English proficiency. The answer that should worry you is: we look for candidates who are comfortable in English. That tells you nothing.

The answer you want is a structured assessment: a conversational interview conducted entirely in English by a native or near-native speaker, an evaluation of written communication through email or async scenarios, and a clear passing bar. You want to know what happens to a candidate who is technically excellent but struggles to communicate in English. A good partner rejects them. A body shop passes them anyway because they need to fill the role.

The Guarantee Question

What happens if a placement does not work out? The minimum acceptable answer is a replacement guarantee. The good answer includes specific details: what is the timeframe (30 days? 90 days?), what qualifies as a failed placement, and what the replacement process looks like.

Some partners offer free replacements within 90 days with no questions asked. Others have fine print that makes the guarantee hard to trigger in practice. Ask for the guarantee terms in writing before you sign anything.

Also ask: in what percentage of their placements have they actually used the replacement guarantee? A company that has never had to replace a placement is either very good or not tracking it. A company that has a small but real replacement rate and can tell you how they handled it is a company that has built the process to make it right when things go wrong.

The Ongoing Support Question

Nearshore staffing is not a one-time transaction. After placement, the engineer is working inside your team and you need a partner who can support that ongoing relationship. Ask what happens after the engineer starts.

A serious answer includes: regular check-ins at 30, 60, and 90 days, a named account manager who knows both you and the engineer, a clear escalation path if something is not working, and support for compensation reviews and role evolution as the relationship matures.

A concerning answer is: we place them and then your ongoing relationship is with the engineer directly. That might sound like what you want, but it means you have no structural support if the relationship hits friction.

Red Flags

Beyond the specific questions, some things should raise immediate concern regardless of what else you are told.

A partner who cannot give you specific examples of clients in your industry or at your stage is a generalist with no depth. A partner who guarantees a candidate in 48 hours is almost certainly pulling from a pre-existing pool without doing fresh sourcing for your role. A partner who does not push back on your requirements, who just says yes to everything, is one who will send you candidates who do not actually match. A good partner tells you when a role will be hard to fill, why, and what adjustments might help.

Finally, ask for a reference from a client who is similar to you in stage and industry. Talk to that reference and ask: did the placements work out? How did the partner handle it when things did not go as planned? Would you use them again?

The answers to those questions will tell you more than anything in a sales presentation.
